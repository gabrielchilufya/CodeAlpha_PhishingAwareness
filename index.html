How attackers manipulate you

Phishing works on people, not computers. This is social engineering: using emotion to bypass logic.

Urgency

"Act now or lose access." Pressure removes time to think.

Authority

Pretending to be your boss, bank, IT team, or government.

Fear

Threats of fines, locked accounts, or legal action.

Greed and curiosity

Prizes, refunds, "see who viewed your profile".

Trust

Pretexting: a believable story, often with details found on social media.

Other channels

SMS (smishing), phone calls (vishing), QR codes, fake login sites.

Real-world examples

Target, 2013Attackers phished a heating and cooling vendor, used its credentials to get into Target's network, and stole payment card data from millions of customers.
Ubiquiti, 2015Fraudulent emails impersonating executives tricked staff into wiring about $46 million to attacker-controlled accounts. This is business email compromise.
Google Docs, 2017A fake "shared document" email asked people to grant a malicious app access to their Gmail, spreading to their contacts within hours.
Twitter, 2020Attackers phoned employees posing as internal IT, got their credentials, and took over high-profile accounts to run a bitcoin scam.

How to protect yourself

  • Check the full sender address and hover over links before clicking.
  • Go to websites by typing the address or using a bookmark, not an emailed link.
  • Never share passwords, OTP codes, or PINs, since legitimate staff never ask.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication on every important account.
  • Use a password manager. It will not autofill on fake domains.
  • Verify unusual requests (payments, gift cards) through a second channel, like a phone call.
  • Report suspicious messages to your IT or security team, then delete them.
  • If you clicked something, change your password immediately and tell someone.
